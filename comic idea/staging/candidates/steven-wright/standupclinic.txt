What’s a one-liner?
A one-liner is a concise and witty statement that aims to evoke humor or make a clever observation. It typically consists of a single line or sentence, hence the name &#8220;one-liner.&#8221; What sets a one-liner apart from a riddle or a joke is its brevity and directness. Here are some distinguishing features:
One-liners are short and to the point, often delivering the punchline immediately without much buildup or storytelling.
They rely on clever wordplay, double meanings, or unexpected twists to create humor. The humor is often derived from a clever observation about everyday life or a surprising connection between ideas.
They are structured as a single sentence or line
, making them quick and easy to deliver. This format contrasts with jokes, which may involve a setup and punchline, and riddles, which pose a question or problem to be solved.
One-liners aim for an immediate laugh or reaction, often relying on the audience&#8217;s quick recognition of the humor or irony present in the statement.
In essence, while jokes and riddles can also be witty and humorous, one-liners distinguish themselves by their economy of words and directness in delivering humor or making a clever observation.
Jokes can have a set-up and a punchline while a one liner achieves the joke without the set up. They are very clever because of that. Steven Wright does throw in the occasional line that has a set-up and a punchline, but his non-sequitur observational jokes are the ones that really make me laugh.
In studying the technique you can begin to identify not only the structure of the joke but how Wright put the joke together. You&#8217;ll notice that a lot of his earlier jokes were derived from taking a idiom or cliché, and reforming the ending. This is one of the oldest approaches in the book, but it&#8217;s still very effective.
So take a look at the lines, then look at the video compilation to see how Wright blends his deadpan character to create a bit of a mystery behind the character.
One of the things you&#8217;ll notice about Steven Wright&#8217;s deliver is that he never looks to the audience for their approval. He remains in this state that could be best described as bewilderment, frustration and confusion. It&#8217;s a technique Jerry Seinfeld describes as &#8220;staying in the bit.&#8221;
